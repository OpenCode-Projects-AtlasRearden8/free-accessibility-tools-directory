Free Accessibility Tools

Free ways to make your computer, phone, and browser easier on your eyes, ears, hands, reading, and focus - screen readers, magnifiers, dictation, captions, dyslexia-friendly fonts, keyboard helpers, and the accessibility features already hiding in your devices. Pick what you need below, look for the green "Start here" verdict, and open the official link to get going.

Companion resource to the Free Large-Print Tech Kit, which covers big-type phone and computer basics for less-techy relatives; this directory reaches into the wider world of free assistive tools and device settings.

  1. Pick what you want help with below: seeing, hearing, hands, reading, focus, or speech.
  2. Open the official link on a card and try the tool on something that annoys you every day.
  3. Keep whatever helps you. Everything here is free, so experimenting costs nothing.

Every card states exactly why the tool is free, links only to its official home, and shows the date it was last hand-checked - 2026-10-27 this pass, with the whole list scheduled for re-verification by 2027-01-27. Built-ins are the fastest wins because they are already on the device you own.

Screen readers

Hear the screen: menus, messages, and buttons spoken aloud so you can navigate without seeing it.

Apple VoiceOver

Start hereBuilt in

The screen reader already built into every iPhone, iPad, and Mac.

VoiceOver speaks whatever is under your finger or keyboard focus - labels, messages, buttons, photos - so you can drive an iPhone with gestures or a Mac with the keyboard. Siri can switch it on with a single phrase, and it includes a built-in practice area where gestures can be tried without disturbing anything.

Start hereIf your device is an iPhone, iPad, or Mac, use the screen reader you already own before installing anything. Gesture navigation takes a few days to feel natural - the built-in VoiceOver Practice mode exists for exactly that.

  • Why it is freeIncluded with iOS, iPadOS, and macOS at no cost. No purchase, subscription, or unlock fee exists for it, and Apple documents it thoroughly in its free support guides.
  • Works oniPhone / iPad, Mac
  • Version and limitsBuilt into iOS, iPadOS, and macOS; braille displays supported

NVDA (NonVisual Desktop Access)

Start hereOpen source

A complete talking screen reader for Windows that costs nothing, not even a license key.

NVDA reads out what is on your Windows screen - menus, buttons, your inbox, web pages, documents - so you can run a computer entirely by ear. It works with braille displays, dozens of languages and voices, and every major browser and office suite.

Start hereIf you use Windows and need a screen reader, start here: install NVDA and speech begins within minutes. Give yourself one unhurried afternoon to learn the navigation basics, and the certification or onboarding videos from NV Access are free too.

  • Why it is freeThe full source code is published under the GNU GPL v2 open-source license, and NV Access, the nonprofit that develops NVDA, never charges for the software. It funds NVDA with donations; the installer includes a friendly message encouraging a donation that you can decline.
  • Works onWindows PC
  • Version and limitsWindows 10 and 11
  • LicenseGNU GPL v2 (free and open source)

Orca

Easy winOpen source

The free, open-source screen reader that ships with GNOME-based Linux.

Orca provides speech and refreshable-braille access to the Linux graphical desktop, working with LibreOffice, Firefox, Thunderbird, and other apps that support the AT-SPI accessibility layer. On GNOME, Super + Alt + S toggles it; a preferences window tunes voices, rate, and key bindings.

Easy winIf your distribution uses GNOME (Ubuntu and Fedora are the gentlest), Orca is already installed - the shortcut is Super + Alt + S. Other desktops need a little more setup, so pick a GNOME-based distro if you are choosing new.

  • Why it is freeDeveloped by the GNOME Project as free software and included in major distributions (Ubuntu, Fedora, and friends) - no cost, no paid edition, and the official site states plainly that it is 'a free, open source... screen reader'.

Magnifiers

Enlarge whatever is on screen, from a small following lens to the whole display.

iPhone & iPad Magnifier

Start hereBuilt in

A store-bought magnifying glass replacement: camera zoom with brightness, contrast, filters, and text detection.

Open Magnifier (or ask Siri) and the rear camera becomes a zoom lens you can enlarge, brighten, and boost contrast on - menus, price tags, medication labels. Its detection features can find and read text in the camera view, and filters help with color discrimination.

Start hereFive seconds to try: tell Siri 'Open Magnifier.' It is one of the most loved everyday iOS features for people who, say, want to read a tiny serial number or a prescription label.

  • Why it is freeInstalled with iOS and iPadOS on the devices Apple lists (a current iPhone makes it appear as a normal app; it can also live in Control Center). No purchase, no sign-in beyond your existing device, no ads.

Start hereTwo shortcuts (Windows + Plus to zoom in, Windows + Esc to stop) do most of the work. Softer zoom settings and cursor thickness live in Settings - Accessibility - Magnifier once you are ready.

  • Why it is freePart of Windows 10 and 11 - no download, no account, no paid tier. Microsoft documents it freely in its support library alongside a keyboard-shortcut cheat sheet.

macOS Voice Control & Dictation

Easy winBuilt in

Two free ways to work by voice on a Mac: speech-to-text everywhere, or full computer control.

Dictation (System Settings - Keyboard) writes into any text field whenever you press the mic key or dictate keyword. Easy winTry plain Dictation first, then Voice Control once you want to move around without your hands. One quirk to know: while Voice Control is on, it - not plain Dictation - handles the typing.

Keyboard-only helpers

Run the whole computer without reaching for the mouse.

Windows Sticky Keys & Filter Keys

Start hereBuilt in

Press shortcut combos one key at a time, and make the keyboard ignore stray bumps and repeats.

Sticky Keys (five presses of Shift toggles it) lets you press the keys in Ctrl + S one at a time; Filter Keys (hold Right Shift eight seconds) ignores brief or repeated keystrokes so a shaky hand does not type five letters for one. Both live in Settings - Accessibility - Keyboard, with toggles to silence or disable the keyboard shortcuts that switch them.

Start hereOne-handed or tremor-affected typists feel the benefit on day one. Turn on both, then look at the same settings page to switch off the Shift-tap shortcuts if they switch these helpers on by accident.

  • Why it is freeIncluded in Windows 10 and 11; Microsoft documents every toggle on its 'make your mouse, keyboard, and other input devices easier to use' support page at no charge.
  • Works onWindows PC
  • Version and limitsWindows 10 and 11

Vimium

Worth practicingOpen source

A keyboard extension for browsers: scroll with j and k, click any visible link by typing the letter it labels.

Vimium adds one-key web navigation in the spirit of the Vim editor: j/k scrolls, f draws a letter tag on every clickable item, and single keys copy URLs, switch tabs, and scroll by screens. Key bindings are fully remappable and the help dialog (type ?) lists yours.

Worth practicingLife-changing for committed keyboard-only users; a steep first week for everyone else. Start with just j, k, and f, ignore the tab commands for now, and bookmark the official shortcut list from the site.

  • Why it is freeThe source code is published under the MIT license on the project's own GitHub repository, and the extension installs free from the Chrome Web Store and Firefox Add-ons - no tier of payment exists.

Simpler phones and everyday settings

Accessibility modes and settings Apple and Google already ship with the phone.

iPhone Assistive Access

Start hereBuilt in

A radically simplified iPhone interface - giant labels, a short app list - for people who find a full phone overwhelming.

Assistive Access (iOS 17 and later) replaces the home screen with a large grid or row-style list of only the chosen apps - calls, messages, camera, photos, music - in very large text with a clear, simple structure. A trusted supporter sets it up, personalizes it, and can enter or exit it.

Start hereConsider it for a parent or relative who finds a busy modern phone stressful: it keeps the essential apps and hides everything else. Setup takes ten minutes - take it slow picking which apps make the cut.

  • Why it is freePart of iOS itself; Apple publishes the complete Assistive Access user guide free of charge, with no sign-up to read it.
  • Works oniPhone / iPad
  • Version and limitsiOS 17 and later

Android Sound Amplifier

Easy winBuilt in

Headphones become a hearing helper: loud, clean sound from the room, with noise filtered down.

Sound Amplifier boosts and clarifies the sound around you through wired or Bluetooth headphones - useful in restaurants, meetings, or lectures - and can separate speech from background noise. It also amplifies media playing on the phone itself.

Easy winTry it before anyone assumes more hardware is needed for casual listening. It is not a substitute for an audiologist - if hearing loss is a real concern, an actual hearing test is the next step, and this can still help in between.

  • Why it is freeA free Android accessibility feature from Google, documented in Android Accessibility Help; on many phones it is preinstalled and elsewhere downloads free from Google Play.

Apple Live Speech & Personal Voice

Easy winBuilt in

Type a sentence and the device speaks it during calls and in person - even in a voice built to sound like you.

Live Speech speaks typed text through FaceTime, Phone calls, and the device speaker, with saved favorite phrases for one-tap replay. Personal Voice goes further: record a training session and your device builds a synthesized voice that sounds like you, usable in Live Speech and other built-in speech features.

Easy winA kind, zero-cost answer to losing or lacking a speaking voice. Recording a Personal Voice takes some setup time and is limited to certain languages and devices - check Apple's list before promising anyone it will sound like them.

  • Why it is freeIncluded with iOS 17 and later, iPadOS 17 and later, and macOS Sonoma and later; Apple's public articles list the device requirements (iPhone XS or later for Live Speech, iPhone 12 or later for Personal Voice) and never charge for either.
  • Works oniPhone / iPad, Mac

iPhone AssistiveTouch

Easy winBuilt in

A floating on-screen button that performs the presses, pinches, and shakes a hand cannot.

Turn it on in Settings - Accessibility - Touch - AssistiveTouch: a small draggable menu button gives one-tap access to Home, screenshots, volume, rotation lock, one-tap access to Home, screenshots, volume, rotation lock, and multifinger gestures, and it also works with compatible joystick-style accessories. Android Switch Access

Worth practicingBuilt in

Run the whole phone from one or two buttons, a USB switch, or even facial gestures read through the camera.

Switch Access scans the screen and highlights items in turn; pressing your assigned switch selects the highlighted item. It supports external switches, Bluetooth and USB keyboards acting as switches, and Camera Switches that map simple facial gestures to actions - a serious option for people who cannot touch the screen reliably.

Worth practicingPowerful and genuinely life-changing when touch is not an option, but plan on a helper for the first setup - it is a project, not a five-minute toggle. Start with the Setup Guide in Settings - Accessibility - Switch Access and mount the phone securely.

  • Why it is freeFree and built into Android, including the Camera Switches feature; Google documents the setup guide, tips, and settings in its public Accessibility Help pages.
